    Peptide Therapy Explained: BPC-157, CJC-1295, NAD+ and What the Evidence Says

    Oakwell MD care team · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up proteins. Your body makes thousands of them, including insulin and GLP-1 itself. In wellness clinics, the word usually refers to a smaller group of injectable or nasal compounds marketed for recovery or aging. Evidence for them varies widely. Some are FDA-approved for narrow uses, and many are not approved for anything.

    Approval status matters for a practical reason. Approval means controlled trials showed the drug works for a defined use and the factory meets federal standards. A compounded peptide skips that process. That does not make it useless, but it does mean the evidence behind it and the quality checks on each batch are thinner than for an approved drug.

    BPC-157

    BPC-157 is a lab-made fragment of a protein found in stomach juice. Animal studies suggest it may speed healing of tendons and the gut lining, but there are almost no published controlled trials in humans. It is not FDA-approved. The FDA has listed it among bulk substances that raise significant safety concerns for compounding, citing limited human safety data. Rules here have shifted in recent years, so ask your provider about its current status.

    People usually take BPC-157 as a daily injection under the skin near the injured area, or as an oral capsule. Clinic reports mention injection-site redness and lightheadedness, though no one has tracked side effects in a formal study. There is also a theoretical concern. Animal data show it encourages new blood vessel growth, which raises questions for anyone with a cancer history.

    CJC-1295, ipamorelin and sermorelin

    These compounds prompt the pituitary gland to release more of your own growth hormone. Sermorelin was once FDA-approved for children with growth hormone deficiency and was later discontinued by its maker for business reasons, and it is now available only through compounding. CJC-1295 and ipamorelin have never been FDA-approved. Most of the fat loss and sleep claims rest on small, short studies. Side effects can include water retention and higher blood sugar, and flushing at the injection site is common.

    Growth hormone releasers can affect blood sugar and are usually avoided in people with active cancer. Expect an IGF-1 blood test at baseline and a repeat after two or three months to see whether levels rose too far. Injections are often taken at bedtime. Most protocols run in cycles.

    Tesamorelin is the exception. Sold as Egrifta, it carries FDA approval for reducing abdominal fat in people with HIV-related lipodystrophy. Using it for general weight loss or aging in people without that condition is off-label, and insurance almost never covers that use. A provider should check blood sugar before and during treatment.

    NAD+

    NAD+ is a coenzyme every cell uses to turn food into energy, and levels drop with age. Clinics offer it by IV drip, injection or nasal spray, with claims about energy and slowing aging. Human data is limited. Oral precursors such as nicotinamide riboside raise blood NAD+ levels in studies, though clear real-world benefits have not been proven. Fast IV infusions commonly cause flushing and chest tightness, so they are usually run slowly.

    NAD+ injections are usually given a few times a week at first, then less often. They are not FDA-approved for any condition, and most of the excitement comes from animal research on aging and cell energy rather than from human trials. Expect a mild flush.

    Questions to ask before starting a peptide

    • Is this product FDA-approved, and for what?
    • What human studies support the use I am interested in?
    • Which licensed pharmacy compounds it, and is it a 503A or 503B facility?
    • What side effects should I watch for, and what labs will you check?
    • How long will I take it, and how will we know if it works?

    Skip anything sold online as research use only. Those products are not made for human use, and purity testing is often missing. Bremelanotide, sold as Vyleesi, is an FDA-approved peptide for low sexual desire in premenopausal women, a reminder that a peptide can be approved once proper trials are completed.
