    GLP-1 Side Effects in the First Month and How to Manage Them

    Oakwell MD care team · October 6, 2026 · 6 min read

    Most GLP-1 side effects show up in the first four to eight weeks, and most of them live in the stomach. Nausea is the most common complaint, and constipation comes in right behind it. Sulfur burps happen too. These effects tend to spike for a few days after the first dose and after each dose increase, then ease as the body adapts to slower stomach emptying.

    Why the first month is the hardest

    GLP-1 medications slow the rate at which food leaves your stomach. Food lingers in the stomach longer than it used to, and that lingering is part of the fullness effect. Greasy meals can backfire. In the first weeks your gut is recalibrating to that slower pace. Titration exists for this reason: starting doses such as 0.25 mg of semaglutide or 2.5 mg of tirzepatide are meant to let you adapt, not to drive weight loss.

    Timing your injection helps some people. Taking the shot in the evening lets the worst of the queasiness happen while you sleep. Others pick a Friday so the rough day falls on a weekend, when a slower pace is easier to manage. Experiment a little. Write down which day and time you injected and how you felt over the next 72 hours, then show that log to your provider at the next check-in.

    Managing nausea and stomach upset

    Small changes at the table do most of the work. Patients who do best tend to follow a handful of habits, particularly in the 48 to 72 hours after a shot, which is when nausea tends to be strongest.

    • Eat smaller meals and stop at the first sign of fullness.
    • Go easy on fried and greasy foods, especially at dinner.
    • Sip water through the day instead of drinking a lot with meals.
    • Try bland foods like crackers or plain rice when queasy.
    • Avoid lying down for two to three hours after eating.

    Ginger tea or ginger chews help some people. Vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids for a full day is a reason to message your provider, since dehydration matters more than the nausea itself. Your provider can also slow the titration, holding you at the current dose for another four weeks before stepping up. Some prescribe an anti-nausea medication such as ondansetron for the rough days.

    Food aversions are normal in the first weeks. Red meat and coffee are the two that patients mention most often, though most of those aversions ease once the dose is stable.

    Constipation, diarrhea and reflux

    Constipation catches people off guard because they are eating less. Aim for roughly 25 to 30 grams of fiber a day and at least eight cups of fluid, more if you exercise or live somewhere hot. A short walk after meals helps. Many providers are fine with an over-the-counter stool softener or polyethylene glycol, though belly pain or several days without a bowel movement deserve a message first.

    Diarrhea is less common and usually brief. Sugar alcohols in protein bars and diet snacks are a frequent trigger, so check labels and keep up with fluids and electrolytes. Reflux and heartburn can also appear because food stays in the stomach longer, so eating your last meal two to three hours before bed often helps.

    Fatigue, headaches and when to call

    Some people feel tired or get headaches in the first weeks, often because calorie and fluid intake dropped faster than they realized. Eating enough protein helps, along with steady fluids and not skipping meals entirely. Dizziness on standing usually means low fluids.

    Hair shedding sometimes starts around month two or three. It is usually a temporary condition called telogen effluvium, triggered by rapid weight loss and low protein rather than by the drug directly. Aim for at least 60 grams of protein a day early on. Ask your provider about checking iron and vitamin D levels.

    Call your provider right away, or seek urgent care, for any of these warning signs. Do not wait these out. Intense upper abdominal pain, especially pain that bores through to the back, can be a sign of pancreatitis. Pain under the right ribs with fever, or skin and eyes turning yellow, can signal a gallbladder problem that needs to be checked the same day. Swelling of the face or throat or any trouble breathing calls for 911. Persistent vomiting with very little urine is another reason not to wait.
